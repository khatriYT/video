Tomorrow morning, the world wakes up to a strange kind of silence. Every bee on Earth is gone. Not just honeybees. All twenty thousand species, from big fuzzy bumblebees to tiny bees barely two millimetres long. The hives are empty. The flowers are still blooming, but nobody's coming. You've probably heard what happens next. There's a famous quote, usually credited to Albert Einstein: If the bee disappeared off the face of the Earth, man would only have four years of life left. It's dramatic. It's everywhere. And there's no evidence Einstein ever said it. So what would really happen? Would we starve? Or is the truth stranger than that? Let's find out. What if every bee on Earth disappeared?

First, forget the picture of a bee you have in your head. Honeybees are just one kind of bee, and in most places they're not really wild. They're kept by people, a bit like livestock. Most of the world's bees live alone. They don't make honey, they don't have a queen, and many nest in tiny tunnels in the ground. There are bumblebees, big and fuzzy, that can fly even in the cold. Mason bees, that build walls of mud. Leafcutter bees, that line their nests with perfect circles cut from leaves. And together, all of them do one job better than almost anything else on the planet: they move pollen.

A single honeybee colony can hold tens of thousands of bees, almost all of them sisters, the daughters of one queen. When a forager finds a good patch of flowers, she flies home and performs a waggle dance. The angle of the dance points the way, and its length tells her sisters how far to fly. To make a single jar of honey, a colony's bees have to visit millions of flowers. An incredible delivery network. But it's just one species out of twenty thousand.

A flower can't walk over to another flower. To make seeds and fruit, most plants need pollen carried from one flower to another. So they pay for delivery. Sweet nectar for energy, and pollen packed with protein. A bee's fuzzy body is covered in branched hairs that grab pollen like velcro. Many even carry pollen baskets on their back legs. Bees can even see ultraviolet patterns on petals that we can't, like landing lights guiding them in. Scientists estimate that nearly nine in ten flowering plant species rely on animals to be pollinated. Bees aren't the only animals doing this job. But they're the biggest workforce.

Bees and flowers have been working together for a very long time. Scientists think bees evolved from hunting wasps that switched from feeding their young on insects to feeding them pollen. Flowers that offered better rewards got more visits. Bees that carried more pollen raised more young. Over something like a hundred million years, they shaped each other. That's why so many flowers are bright, sweet smelling and shaped like a perfect landing pad for a bee.

So, back to that silent morning. At first, almost nothing seems different. The trees are green. The grass still grows. Grass doesn't need bees at all. Neither do wheat, rice or corn. Their pollen is carried by the wind. And those three grains alone provide around half of all the calories people eat. So no, humanity would not starve in four years. But walk into a supermarket, and things start to look very different.

In a landmark study, scientists looked at the world's 115 most important crops. 87 of them benefit from animal pollination. Apples. Cherries. Blueberries. Strawberries. Almonds. Avocados. Melons. Pumpkins. Coffee. Pollinator-dependent crops make up around a third of the world's food production. Now imagine those shelves without them. Apples and cherries would become rare luxuries. Almonds would almost vanish. Chocolate, funnily enough, would be fine. Cocoa flowers are pollinated mostly by tiny midges. Bread, rice, pasta and potatoes would still be there. We'd survive. But our meals would become far more boring, and far less healthy.

Here's the part most people miss. Fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds are packed with vitamins and minerals. In 2015, researchers modelled what would happen to human health if every animal pollinator vanished. Their estimate: about one point four million extra deaths every year, from heart disease, strokes, some cancers and nutrient deficiencies. The same model found that tens of millions more people could fall short of vitamin A, which protects eyesight, and folate, which helps babies develop. That's for losing all animal pollinators, not just bees. But bees are the biggest part of that workforce. It wouldn't be a sudden famine. It would be a slow, quiet health crisis.

To see how much we already depend on bees, look at California every February. Around one point four million acres of almond trees burst into bloom almost at once. It's often called the biggest pollination event on Earth. There aren't nearly enough local bees. So beekeepers truck in hives from all across the country. In 2024, around two point seven million colonies were needed. That's tens of billions of bees, rented for a few weeks. When the almond bloom ends, many of those hives are loaded up again and driven to apples, cherries and blueberries further north. Without bees, these orchards would produce almost no almonds at all.

Is there a backup plan? In some places, people are already doing the bees' job. In parts of Sichuan, China, heavy pesticide use helped wipe out local pollinators. So farmers climbed into their apple and pear trees and pollinated the blossoms by hand. With a brush made of feathers on a stick, one flower at a time. It works. But it's slow and expensive. One study found that using bees would be about eight times cheaper. Now imagine doing that for every apple tree, every melon field and every blueberry bush on Earth.

What about robots? Engineers have built tiny flying robots and pollinating drones. They're impressive, but nowhere near ready to replace billions of bees. Bees do it for a sip of nectar.

So could other insects take over? Partly. Flies, especially hoverflies, already make a big share of the visits to crop flowers. One study found other insects make around four in ten of them. Beetles, butterflies and moths pollinate too. Some plants would find new partners. But many flowers are built for bees, and no other group comes close to their numbers or their tireless work.

Farms are only half the story. In the wild, the loss of bees would ripple outward. Wildflowers that rely on bees would make fewer seeds, year after year. Some plants hide their pollen inside tiny tubes. To get it out, bees use a trick called buzz pollination. A bumblebee grabs the flower and vibrates its flight muscles, shaking the pollen out like salt from a shaker. Honeybees can't do this. Tomatoes, blueberries and many wild plants get much better harvests with these buzzing visitors. Greenhouse growers used to shake their tomato plants with hand-held vibrators. Today, many simply buy boxes of bumblebees instead. As those plants decline, the seeds and berries that birds and mammals feed on would thin out. And bees are food themselves. Bee-eaters, spiders, dragonflies and wasps would all lose a meal. Nature would adapt. But some plants, and the animals that depend on them, could disappear along with the bees.

Even things you'd never connect to bees would feel it. Alfalfa, a major feed for dairy cows, is grown from seed that bees pollinate. Cotton, sunflowers and canola all give bigger harvests when bees visit. So bees touch your clothes, your milk and the oil in your kitchen.

Now here's the uncomfortable twist. This isn't only a thought experiment. Many bees really are in trouble. Beekeepers in many countries lose a large share of their colonies every winter. In 2006, American beekeepers reported something eerie: colonies where the worker bees had simply vanished, leaving the queen and the honey behind. It was named colony collapse disorder. A tiny parasite called the Varroa mite feeds on bees and spreads viruses between them. Some pesticides, including a group called neonicotinoids, can harm bees. In 2018, the European Union banned outdoor use of three of them. Wild bees are losing their homes, as meadows and hedgerows are replaced by bare fields and concrete. And a changing climate can throw flowers and bees out of sync, so the flowers bloom before the bees are ready. Most scientists agree there's no single villain. It's all of these pressures stacking up together.

The good news? Bees can recover when they're given room to live. Planting native wildflowers, leaving part of the garden a little messy, and using fewer pesticides all help. Many solitary bees nest in bare soil or hollow stems. A bundle of stems can become a bee hotel. Even a window box of flowers can be a refuelling stop for a passing bee. Farmers who leave strips of wildflowers beside their fields often get better pollination for their crops. And some cities now plant flowering roadsides and rooftops just for pollinators.

So, what would happen if all bees disappeared? We wouldn't all die in four years. That quote was never real. But our plates would turn beige, our health would suffer, and millions of people could pay the price. Farmers would climb into trees with feather brushes. Life would go on. But it would be a poorer, plainer and quieter world. And meadows around the world would grow a little quieter, a little emptier, every spring. Bees aren't just honey makers. They're the delivery service that keeps much of the living world, and our kitchens, running. And right now, they could use a little help from us.
