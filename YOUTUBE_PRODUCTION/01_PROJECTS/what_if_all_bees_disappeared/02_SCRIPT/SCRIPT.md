Tomorrow morning, the world wakes up to a strange kind of silence.

Every bee on Earth is gone. Not just honeybees. All twenty thousand species, from big fuzzy bumblebees to tiny bees barely two millimetres long.

The hives are empty.  
*[VISUAL, scene 003: A beekeeper in a white suit and veil lifts the lid of a hive: empty honeycomb frames, no bees.]*

The flowers are still blooming, but nobody's coming.  
*[VISUAL, scene 004: Close row of open flowers turned toward the sky, waiting; a ticking clock overlay; no insects anywhere.]*

You've probably heard what happens next. There's a famous quote, usually credited to Albert Einstein:  
*[VISUAL, scene 005: An old cork board with a yellowed quote card pinned to it, next to a sketch of a wild-haired scientist silhouette.]*

If the bee disappeared off the face of the Earth, man would only have four years of life left.

It's dramatic. It's everywhere. And there's no evidence Einstein ever said it.  
*[VISUAL, scene 007: A big red stamp slams onto the quote card.]*

So what would really happen? Would we starve? Or is the truth stranger than that?  
*[VISUAL, scene 008: Pip in a kitchen opening a fridge full of colourful food, thinking.]*

Let's find out.  
*[VISUAL, scene 009: Pip grabs the mustard bucket hat and satchel at the cottage door.]*

What if every bee on Earth disappeared?  
*[VISUAL, scene 010: Pip on a hill above a patchwork of farmland and orchards at morning, title appears.]*

## MEET THE BEES

First, forget the picture of a bee you have in your head.  
*[VISUAL, scene 011: Pip holds up a drawing of a classic striped honeybee; it gets crossed out.]*

Honeybees are just one kind of bee, and in most places they're not really wild. They're kept by people, a bit like livestock.  
*[VISUAL, scene 012: A neat apiary like a small farm: rows of colourful hive boxes, a beekeeper with a smoker, a fence and a barn.]*

Most of the world's bees live alone. They don't make honey, they don't have a queen, and many nest in tiny tunnels in the ground.  
*[VISUAL, scene 013: Cut-away soil cross-section under a meadow: tunnels with pollen balls and single bees in each chamber.]*

There are bumblebees, big and fuzzy, that can fly even in the cold.  
*[VISUAL, scene 014: A fat fuzzy bumblebee flies over frosty mountain flowers, breath-like steam, snow-capped peaks.]*

Mason bees, that build walls of mud.  
*[VISUAL, scene 015: A mason bee seals a hollow reed tube with a little wall of mud.]*

Leafcutter bees, that line their nests with perfect circles cut from leaves.  
*[VISUAL, scene 016: A leafcutter bee cuts a neat circle from a leaf and flies off with it.]*

And together, all of them do one job better than almost anything else on the planet: they move pollen.  
*[VISUAL, scene 017: A meadow with dozens of different bees flying flower to flower, leaving glowing yellow pollen trails.]*

## INSIDE THE HIVE

A single honeybee colony can hold tens of thousands of bees, almost all of them sisters, the daughters of one queen.  
*[VISUAL, scene 018: Cut-away of a hive box showing golden honeycomb frames packed with bees; the larger queen bee in the centre with a tiny crown.]*

When a forager finds a good patch of flowers, she flies home and performs a waggle dance. The angle of the dance points the way, and its length tells her sisters how far to fly.  
*[VISUAL, scene 019: On the honeycomb, a bee runs a figure-eight dance with a waggle line; an arrow and a sun icon show the direction to a flower patch.]*

To make a single jar of honey, a colony's bees have to visit millions of flowers.  
*[VISUAL, scene 020: A honey jar fills drop by drop while a counter of flowers spins up beside it.]*

An incredible delivery network. But it's just one species out of twenty thousand.  
*[VISUAL, scene 021: Zoom out from one hive to a map of countless bee icons of different shapes.]*

## HOW POLLINATION WORKS

A flower can't walk over to another flower.  
*[VISUAL, scene 022: Two flowers on opposite sides of a garden path, leaning toward each other but rooted.]*

To make seeds and fruit, most plants need pollen carried from one flower to another.  
*[VISUAL, scene 023: Diagram on paper: pollen grains travel along a dotted arrow from one flower to another, then a little fruit grows.]*

So they pay for delivery. Sweet nectar for energy, and pollen packed with protein.  
*[VISUAL, scene 024: A flower as a shop counter offering a nectar droplet and pollen to a bee customer.]*

A bee's fuzzy body is covered in branched hairs that grab pollen like velcro. Many even carry pollen baskets on their back legs.  
*[VISUAL, scene 025: Extreme close-up of a honeybee covered in pollen grains with two bulging yellow pollen baskets.]*

Bees can even see ultraviolet patterns on petals that we can't, like landing lights guiding them in.  
*[VISUAL, scene 026: Split view of the same flower: left plain yellow, right in UV view with glowing target pattern.]*

Scientists estimate that nearly nine in ten flowering plant species rely on animals to be pollinated.  
*[VISUAL, scene 027: Ten flower icons in a row; nine light up gold.]*

Bees aren't the only animals doing this job. But they're the biggest workforce.  
*[VISUAL, scene 028: Line-up of pollinators: butterfly, moth, hoverfly, beetle, hummingbird, bat; then a huge crowd of bees fills the right side.]*

## A HUNDRED MILLION YEARS TOGETHER

Bees and flowers have been working together for a very long time.  
*[VISUAL, scene 029: Prehistoric landscape with early flowering plants and ferns at golden hour.]*

Scientists think bees evolved from hunting wasps that switched from feeding their young on insects to feeding them pollen.  
*[VISUAL, scene 030: Side by side: a slim hunting wasp carrying a caterpillar, and a fuzzy bee carrying pollen; an arrow between them.]*

Flowers that offered better rewards got more visits. Bees that carried more pollen raised more young. Over something like a hundred million years, they shaped each other.  
*[VISUAL, scene 031: A spiral timeline where flowers get bigger and brighter as bees get fuzzier.]*

That's why so many flowers are bright, sweet smelling and shaped like a perfect landing pad for a bee.  
*[VISUAL, scene 032: A foxglove-like flower with a landing lip; a bee lands perfectly.]*

## THE FIRST MORNING

So, back to that silent morning.  
*[VISUAL, scene 033: The cottage garden again, quiet, no insects.]*

At first, almost nothing seems different. The trees are green. The grass still grows.  
*[VISUAL, scene 034: A park with trees, joggers and grass, everything looks normal.]*

Grass doesn't need bees at all. Neither do wheat, rice or corn. Their pollen is carried by the wind.  
*[VISUAL, scene 035: Golden wheat field with wind lines carrying pollen dust across.]*

And those three grains alone provide around half of all the calories people eat.  
*[VISUAL, scene 036: Three big bowls: rice, a loaf of bread, corn cobs; a pie chart half filled.]*

So no, humanity would not starve in four years. But walk into a supermarket, and things start to look very different.  
*[VISUAL, scene 037: Exterior of a bright supermarket; Pip pushes a trolley inside.]*

## THE SUPERMARKET

In a landmark study, scientists looked at the world's 115 most important crops. 87 of them benefit from animal pollination.  
*[VISUAL, scene 038: A huge produce aisle in perspective, bins overflowing with colourful fruit and vegetables.]*

Apples. Cherries. Blueberries. Strawberries. Almonds. Avocados. Melons. Pumpkins. Coffee.  
*[VISUAL, scene 039: Each item pops onto the screen in a grid as it's named.]*

Pollinator-dependent crops make up around a third of the world's food production.  
*[VISUAL, scene 040: A giant pie chart made of food: one third bright fruit, two thirds beige grains.]*

Now imagine those shelves without them.  
*[VISUAL, scene 041: The same produce aisle; colourful items fade away leaving empty bins.]*

Apples and cherries would become rare luxuries. Almonds would almost vanish.  
*[VISUAL, scene 042: Price tags on a single apple and a few almonds spin upward to absurd prices.]*

Chocolate, funnily enough, would be fine. Cocoa flowers are pollinated mostly by tiny midges.  
*[VISUAL, scene 043: A cocoa pod and a bar of chocolate with a tiny midge waving; mosquito-episode callback badge.]*

Bread, rice, pasta and potatoes would still be there.  
*[VISUAL, scene 044: Shelves of bread loaves, rice sacks, pasta and potatoes, all still stocked.]*

We'd survive. But our meals would become far more boring, and far less healthy.  
*[VISUAL, scene 045: Pip at a table with a plate of plain beige food, sighing.]*

## THE HIDDEN HEALTH CRISIS

Here's the part most people miss. Fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds are packed with vitamins and minerals.  
*[VISUAL, scene 046: Fruits and nuts with little sparkling vitamin badges orbiting them.]*

In 2015, researchers modelled what would happen to human health if every animal pollinator vanished.  
*[VISUAL, scene 047: Scientist B at a computer screen running a model; graphs on the wall.]*

Their estimate: about one point four million extra deaths every year, from heart disease, strokes, some cancers and nutrient deficiencies.  
*[VISUAL, scene 048: A world map with small person icons fading; a big counter.]*

The same model found that tens of millions more people could fall short of vitamin A, which protects eyesight, and folate, which helps babies develop.  
*[VISUAL, scene 049: An eye icon and a baby icon with vitamin badges dimming.]*

That's for losing all animal pollinators, not just bees. But bees are the biggest part of that workforce.  
*[VISUAL, scene 050: A stacked bar: bees large block, other pollinators smaller blocks.]*

It wouldn't be a sudden famine. It would be a slow, quiet health crisis.  
*[VISUAL, scene 051: An hourglass with fruit-shaped grains slowly draining.]*

## THE ALMOND RUSH

To see how much we already depend on bees, look at California every February.  
*[VISUAL, scene 052: Stylised map of California with a calendar showing February.]*

Around one point four million acres of almond trees burst into bloom almost at once. It's often called the biggest pollination event on Earth.  
*[VISUAL, scene 053: Endless rows of white-pink blossoming almond trees under blue sky.]*

There aren't nearly enough local bees. So beekeepers truck in hives from all across the country.  
*[VISUAL, scene 054: A highway with trucks loaded with stacked hive boxes driving toward the orchards.]*

In 2024, around two point seven million colonies were needed. That's tens of billions of bees, rented for a few weeks.  
*[VISUAL, scene 055: Hive boxes unloaded in rows among blooming trees; bees swarming out.]*

When the almond bloom ends, many of those hives are loaded up again and driven to apples, cherries and blueberries further north.  
*[VISUAL, scene 056: A map of the USA with truck routes looping from California to orchards in the north-west and east.]*

Without bees, these orchards would produce almost no almonds at all.  
*[VISUAL, scene 057: Bare almond branches with no nuts; a farmer shrugs.]*

## PEOPLE AS BEES

Is there a backup plan? In some places, people are already doing the bees' job.  
*[VISUAL, scene 058: Pip looks at a feather duster with a raised eyebrow.]*

In parts of Sichuan, China, heavy pesticide use helped wipe out local pollinators. So farmers climbed into their apple and pear trees and pollinated the blossoms by hand.  
*[VISUAL, scene 059: Mountain terraces with fruit trees in white blossom; workers on bamboo ladders.]*

With a brush made of feathers on a stick, one flower at a time.  
*[VISUAL, scene 060: Close-up of a feather brush touching a blossom; pollen puff.]*

It works. But it's slow and expensive. One study found that using bees would be about eight times cheaper.  
*[VISUAL, scene 061: Balance scale: one tiny bee outweighs a pile of coins and a tired worker.]*

Now imagine doing that for every apple tree, every melon field and every blueberry bush on Earth.  
*[VISUAL, scene 062: Zoom out over endless orchards with tiny workers everywhere and a clock spinning.]*

## ROBOT BEES?

What about robots?  
*[VISUAL, scene 063: A tiny robot bee hovers, blinking LEDs.]*

Engineers have built tiny flying robots and pollinating drones. They're impressive, but nowhere near ready to replace billions of bees.  
*[VISUAL, scene 064: Robot bee tethered by a wire to a laptop; a drone fumbles a flower; a sweating engineer.]*

Bees do it for a sip of nectar.  
*[VISUAL, scene 065: A honeybee sips nectar and smiles.]*

## COULD OTHER INSECTS TAKE OVER?

So could other insects take over? Partly.  
*[VISUAL, scene 066: Pip with a clipboard interviewing a line of insects: a hoverfly, a beetle, a butterfly and a moth.]*

Flies, especially hoverflies, already make a big share of the visits to crop flowers. One study found other insects make around four in ten of them.  
*[VISUAL, scene 067: A hoverfly hovering over a crop flower, with ten visit tally marks, four of them highlighted.]*

Beetles, butterflies and moths pollinate too. Some plants would find new partners.  
*[VISUAL, scene 068: A beetle on a magnolia, a butterfly on a zinnia, a moth on a night-blooming flower under the moon.]*

But many flowers are built for bees, and no other group comes close to their numbers or their tireless work.  
*[VISUAL, scene 069: A bee-shaped hole in a flower puzzle that the other insects can't fill.]*

## THE WILD WORLD

Farms are only half the story. In the wild, the loss of bees would ripple outward.  
*[VISUAL, scene 070: Wide panorama from farmland into meadow and forest; a ripple ring spreads across.]*

Wildflowers that rely on bees would make fewer seeds, year after year.  
*[VISUAL, scene 071: Meadow time-lapse: year labels change and flowers thin out.]*

Some plants hide their pollen inside tiny tubes. To get it out, bees use a trick called buzz pollination.  
*[VISUAL, scene 072: Close-up of a tomato flower with tube-shaped anthers.]*

A bumblebee grabs the flower and vibrates its flight muscles, shaking the pollen out like salt from a shaker. Honeybees can't do this.  
*[VISUAL, scene 073: Bumblebee clinging to the flower vibrating with buzz lines; pollen shakes out; a salt shaker icon.]*

Tomatoes, blueberries and many wild plants get much better harvests with these buzzing visitors.  
*[VISUAL, scene 074: Tomato vines and blueberry bushes heavy with fruit.]*

Greenhouse growers used to shake their tomato plants with hand-held vibrators. Today, many simply buy boxes of bumblebees instead.  
*[VISUAL, scene 075: Inside a greenhouse: a worker with an electric wand on one side, a cardboard bumblebee box with bees flying out on the other.]*

As those plants decline, the seeds and berries that birds and mammals feed on would thin out.  
*[VISUAL, scene 076: A songbird and a field mouse looking at a bush with only a few berries.]*

And bees are food themselves. Bee-eaters, spiders, dragonflies and wasps would all lose a meal.  
*[VISUAL, scene 077: A colourful bee-eater bird on a branch, a spider web, a dragonfly; all looking around for bees.]*

Nature would adapt. But some plants, and the animals that depend on them, could disappear along with the bees.  
*[VISUAL, scene 078: A row of dominoes: bee, flower, berry, bird; they topple.]*

## COWS, CLOTHES AND COOKING OIL

Even things you'd never connect to bees would feel it.  
*[VISUAL, scene 079: Pip in a farmyard looking at a cow.]*

Alfalfa, a major feed for dairy cows, is grown from seed that bees pollinate.  
*[VISUAL, scene 080: Purple alfalfa flowers with a leafcutter bee; a cow eats a bale of hay.]*

Cotton, sunflowers and canola all give bigger harvests when bees visit.  
*[VISUAL, scene 081: Fields of cotton bolls, sunflowers and yellow canola side by side.]*

So bees touch your clothes, your milk and the oil in your kitchen.  
*[VISUAL, scene 082: A T-shirt, a milk carton and a bottle of oil pop up, each with a tiny bee badge.]*

## WHY BEES ARE IN TROUBLE

Now here's the uncomfortable twist. This isn't only a thought experiment. Many bees really are in trouble.  
*[VISUAL, scene 083: Pip looks serious in front of a chart line going down.]*

Beekeepers in many countries lose a large share of their colonies every winter.  
*[VISUAL, scene 084: Snowy apiary; a beekeeper opens a hive and finds it empty.]*

In 2006, American beekeepers reported something eerie: colonies where the worker bees had simply vanished, leaving the queen and the honey behind. It was named colony collapse disorder.  
*[VISUAL, scene 085: A beekeeper lifts a frame full of honey with only the queen left; question marks float.]*

A tiny parasite called the Varroa mite feeds on bees and spreads viruses between them.  
*[VISUAL, scene 086: Close-up of a red-brown mite clinging to a bee's back; virus doodles around.]*

Some pesticides, including a group called neonicotinoids, can harm bees. In 2018, the European Union banned outdoor use of three of them.  
*[VISUAL, scene 087: A tractor spraying a field; an EU-style stamp lands.]*

Wild bees are losing their homes, as meadows and hedgerows are replaced by bare fields and concrete.  
*[VISUAL, scene 088: Split: a flowery meadow turns into a parking lot as a bulldozer passes.]*

And a changing climate can throw flowers and bees out of sync, so the flowers bloom before the bees are ready.  
*[VISUAL, scene 089: Two calendars: flowers bloom in March, bee wakes in April; a gap between them.]*

Most scientists agree there's no single villain. It's all of these pressures stacking up together.  
*[VISUAL, scene 090: Stack of blocks labelled MITES, PESTICIDES, HABITAT LOSS, CLIMATE on a tiny bee who strains to hold them.]*

## WHAT WE CAN DO

The good news? Bees can recover when they're given room to live.  
*[VISUAL, scene 091: A bee hotel and a flower bed in a backyard; bees return.]*

Planting native wildflowers, leaving part of the garden a little messy, and using fewer pesticides all help.  
*[VISUAL, scene 092: Pip plants flowers; a patch of long grass and dandelions; a crossed-out spray bottle.]*

Many solitary bees nest in bare soil or hollow stems. A bundle of stems can become a bee hotel.  
*[VISUAL, scene 093: A wooden bee hotel with bamboo stems; a mason bee pokes its head out.]*

Even a window box of flowers can be a refuelling stop for a passing bee.  
*[VISUAL, scene 094: A city window box full of flowers with a bee landing; a little fuel-pump icon.]*

Farmers who leave strips of wildflowers beside their fields often get better pollination for their crops.  
*[VISUAL, scene 095: Aerial-ish view of crop fields lined with colourful wildflower strips.]*

And some cities now plant flowering roadsides and rooftops just for pollinators.  
*[VISUAL, scene 096: A city skyline with green flowering rooftops and bees.]*

## CONCLUSION

So, what would happen if all bees disappeared?  
*[VISUAL, scene 097: Pip in the cottage garden at sunset.]*

We wouldn't all die in four years. That quote was never real.  
*[VISUAL, scene 098: The fake quote card blows away in the wind.]*

But our plates would turn beige, our health would suffer, and millions of people could pay the price.  
*[VISUAL, scene 099: A colourful plate slowly drains to beige.]*

Farmers would climb into trees with feather brushes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 100: Orchard with workers on ladders at dusk.]*

Life would go on. But it would be a poorer, plainer and quieter world.  
*[VISUAL, scene 101: Split: colourful market street fading to grey.]*

And meadows around the world would grow a little quieter, a little emptier, every spring.  
*[VISUAL, scene 102: Meadow fading season by season.]*

Bees aren't just honey makers. They're the delivery service that keeps much of the living world, and our kitchens, running.  
*[VISUAL, scene 103: A bee in a tiny courier cap carrying a pollen parcel from flower to flower.]*

And right now, they could use a little help from us.
