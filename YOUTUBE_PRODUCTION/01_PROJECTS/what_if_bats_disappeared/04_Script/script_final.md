## The white nose

**SC015** (plate: HIB_CHAPTER)  
So what happened in that cave?

**SC016** (plate: HIB_CLUSTER)  
Bats in the northern United States survive winter by hibernating. They hang in cold caves for months, living on the fat they built up in autumn.

**SC017** (plate: HIB_HEART; claims C33)  
A hibernating bat's heart can slow from a few hundred beats a minute to about ten, and its body cools to nearly the temperature of the cave.

**SC018** (plate: HIB_FUNGUS_CU2; claims C02)  
The fungus, called Pseudogymnoascus destructans, loves the cold. It grows on their noses, ears and wings while they sleep.

**SC019** (plate: HIB_WAKE; claims C03)  
Infected bats keep waking up. Every time they do, they burn fat they can't replace.

**SC020** (plate: HIB_SNOW_EXIT)  
By late winter, many have nothing left.

**SC021** (card: decline3; claims C05)  
Little brown bats, northern long-eared bats and tricolored bats fell by more than ninety percent, in under ten years.

**SC022** (card: spread; claims C06)  
And the fungus kept spreading, county by county, state by state. It's now been confirmed in more than forty US states, and parts of Canada.

So how much work were those bats really doing?

## The night gardeners

**SC043** (plate: AGAVE_CHAPTER)  
Pest control is only one of their jobs.

**SC044** (plate: AGAVE_WIDE; claims C21)  
More than five hundred kinds of plants rely on bats to pollinate their flowers.

**SC045** (plate: AGAVE_BAT_CU; claims C22)  
In the deserts of Mexico, the lesser long-nosed bat pushes its face into agave flowers to drink nectar, and comes out dusted with pollen.

**SC046** (plate: AGAVE_FARM; claims C22)  
Agave is the plant tequila comes from. Tequila farms grow agave from clones, so they don't actually need bats to make tequila.

**SC047** (plate: AGAVE_BLUE_HOUR; claims C22)  
But clones have almost no genetic variety. Bat pollination keeps wild agave diverse, and better able to survive new diseases. Some growers now let part of their crop flower, just for the bats.

**SC048** (plate: AGAVE_RECOVERY; claims C35)  
The lesser long-nosed bat is also a comeback story. In 1988, fewer than a thousand were known, at just fourteen roosts. Thirty years later there were about two hundred thousand, and it became the first bat ever taken off the US endangered list because it had recovered.

**SC049** (plate: DURIAN_NIGHT; claims C23)  
In Southeast Asia, durian, the famously smelly king of fruits, is pollinated mainly by cave nectar bats. Farms farther from bat caves get less fruit.

**SC050** (plate: TROPIC_FIG; claims C24)  
And fruit bats plant forests. They eat fruit in one place, and drop the seeds somewhere else, often in open, cleared land.

**SC051** (plate: CLEARING_GROW; claims C24)  
Many of those seeds are pioneer plants: the first trees that turn a bare clearing back into forest.


## Aren't bats dangerous?

**SC052** (plate: MOON_CHAPTER; claims C30)  
Of course, bats have a scary reputation. Some carry viruses that can make people sick.

**SC053** (plate: MOON_SILHOUETTE)  
So why not just get rid of them? People have tried.

**SC054** (card: paper; claims C31, C25)  
In Peru, vampire bats spread rabies to livestock, and colonies have been culled for years. A study of twenty colonies found culling didn't reduce rabies exposure. Where bats were culled now and then, exposure actually went up.

**SC055** (plate: MINE_RETURN; claims C26)  
In Uganda, after miners caught Marburg virus in a gold mine, the bats there were wiped out. Years later, bats moved back in, and more of them carried the virus than before.

**SC056** (card: cull_cycle; claims C32)  
Scientists suspect culling leaves behind more young bats that have never met the virus, so it can spread faster. For people, the safest bat is usually one that's left alone.
