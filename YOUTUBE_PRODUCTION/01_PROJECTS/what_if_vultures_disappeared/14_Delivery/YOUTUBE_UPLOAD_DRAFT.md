## Description

It's mid-morning at the edge of a village in India. A cow has died, and within about half an hour, vultures have cleaned it up. Now imagine they never come.

In the 1990s, South Asia's vultures collapsed by more than 95% in about a decade. Scientists traced it to a cheap cattle painkiller. What followed shows exactly what a world without vultures looks like:
- carcasses that rot for weeks;
- swarms of flies;
- booming stray-dog populations;
- rabies;
- and, according to one major study, around half a million extra human deaths.

Which animal do you think we're quietly losing next? Tell me in the comments.

**CHAPTERS** (from the final edit)

**WHAT'S REAL AND WHAT ISN'T**

The village, the cow and Pip are illustrations. The science is real:
- South Asia's Gyps vultures fell by more than 95% (white-rumped vulture: up to 99.9% by some estimates).
- Diclofenac was proven to be the cause in 2004. India banned it for veterinary use in 2006, and banned ketoprofen and aceclofenac in 2023.
- Experiments in Costa Rica (2025) and Kenya (2012) show carcasses last much longer without vultures, with more flies and more mammal scavengers.
- The rise in feral dogs alongside the vulture collapse is real, but **whether one caused the other is debated**.
- The ~4.7% mortality increase, the ~500,000 extra deaths (2000–2005) and the ~$69 billion a year are **statistical estimates** from Frank & Sudarshan (2024), not a body count.
- The Spain carcass-truck emissions (~77,000 t CO2 a year) are a minimum estimate.
