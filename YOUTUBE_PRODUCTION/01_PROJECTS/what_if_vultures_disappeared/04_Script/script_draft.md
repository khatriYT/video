## Meet the cleanup crew

**SC009** (plate: P13)  
First, meet the cleanup crew.

**SC010** (plate: P10)  
Vultures are big, broad-winged birds built for one job: finding dead animals, and finishing them fast.

**SC011** (card: thermal)  
They barely flap. They ride columns of rising warm air, circling for hours while they scan the ground below.

**SC012** (plate: P01)  
They also watch each other. When one vulture drops toward a carcass, others see it and follow, so a single meal can pull in birds from kilometres around.

**SC013** (plate: P11; claims C14)  
One Rüppell's vulture was hit by a plane at eleven thousand three hundred metres. That's higher than Mount Everest.

**SC014** (plate: P14)  
That bald head isn't a flaw. Bare skin stays cleaner when you feed inside a carcass, and it may help the bird shed heat.

**SC015** (card: gut; claims C13)  
And their stomachs are brutal. Harsh acid and specialised gut bacteria let them eat meat carrying germs like anthrax, which would sicken most other animals.

**SC016** (plate: P02)  
In other words, a vulture isn't just eating garbage. It's taking disease out of circulation.


## The collapse

**SC017** (plate: P15)  
In India, vultures were part of everyday life: on rooftops, on old trees, circling over every town dump.

**SC018** (plate: P17)  
Then, through the 1990s, birdwatchers started noticing empty nests, and dead vultures lying under their roosts.

**SC019** (card: decline; claims C01, C02)  
Surveys showed losses of more than ninety-five percent. For the white-rumped vulture, by some estimates, ninety-nine point nine.

**SC020** (card: suspects)  
Scientists suspected a virus. Pesticides. Something in the water.

**SC021** (plate: P16)  
The real killer was hiding in plain sight, in a medicine cabinet.

**SC022** (plate: P19; claims C03)  
Diclofenac: a cheap painkiller that farmers gave to sick and injured cattle.

**SC023** (card: chain; claims C03)  
When a treated cow died, traces of the drug stayed in its body. Harmless to the cow. Deadly to a vulture.

**SC024** (plate: P17; claims C03)  
Their kidneys failed within days, and a single contaminated carcass could poison dozens of birds at once.

**SC025** (card: grid; claims C20)  
And it took shockingly little. One study calculated that if fewer than one carcass in a hundred carried a lethal dose, that alone could explain the crash.

**SC026** (card: redlist; claims C24)  
Today, three of South Asia's vulture species are listed as critically endangered.

**SC027** (card: timeline; claims C03, C04)  
In 2004, researchers finally proved the link. India banned veterinary diclofenac in 2006.

**SC028** (plate: P12)  
But by then, the sky above India was already almost empty.


## A world without vultures

**SC029** (plate: P03)

**SC030** (plate: P03C)

**SC031** (plate: P04)  
Day three. The flies arrive. Then thousands more.

And dogs carry something vultures never spread: rabies.

**SC040** (plate: P07)

## The bill

**SC041** (card: paper; claims C11)  
So did losing the vultures actually cost human lives? In 2024, two economists, Eyal Frank and Anant Sudarshan, set out to measure it.

**SC042** (card: districts; claims C11)  
They compared districts where vultures used to be common with districts where they never were, before and after the diclofenac years.

**SC043** (card: bignum; claims C11)  
Their finding: in former vulture country, human death rates rose by about four point seven percent.

**SC044** (card: icons2; claims C10)  
Sales of rabies vaccine went up. Water quality got worse, as more carcasses were left to rot near rivers and wells.

**SC045** (card: bignum; claims C11)  
Their estimate: roughly a hundred thousand extra deaths a year. About half a million people between 2000 and 2005.

**SC046** (card: bignum; claims C12)  
That's a statistical estimate, not a body count. But it puts a price on the bird nobody wanted: about sixty-nine billion dollars, every year.

## It's happening again

**SC053** (plate: P21)  
And right now, it's happening again. This time in Africa.

**SC054** (card: africa; claims C18)  
A 2016 study found that eight African vulture species had fallen by an average of sixty-two percent. Seven of them by eighty percent or more, in just three generations.

**SC055** (plate: P22; claims C18)  
The main killer here isn't medicine. It's poison, often laced into carcasses, and the trade in vulture parts for traditional remedies.

**SC056** (card: poison; claims C21)  
Some poachers even poison elephant carcasses on purpose, because circling vultures can lead rangers straight to an illegal kill. In one case in Namibia, around five hundred vultures died at a single carcass.

**SC057** (card: bignum; claims C18)  
Together, those two threats account for about ninety percent of the vulture deaths that get reported.


## Could anything replace them?

**SC058** (plate: P09)  
So if vultures vanished everywhere, would something else simply step in?

**SC059** (card: replacements)  
Partly. Dogs, jackals, hyenas, crows, rats, flies and bacteria would all take a share.

**SC060** (card: checklist; claims C19)  
But none of them do all three things vultures do: spot a carcass from far away, strip it in minutes, and destroy the germs inside it.

**SC061** (plate: P06; claims C06, C07, C19)  
The experiments point the same way. Without vultures, carcasses last longer, and the animals that replace them spread more disease, not less.

**SC062** (card: keystone)  
Ecologists call that a keystone loss: pull out one stone, and the whole arch starts to lean.
