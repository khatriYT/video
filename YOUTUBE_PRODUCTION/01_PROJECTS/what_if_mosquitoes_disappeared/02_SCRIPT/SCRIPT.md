## WHY MOSQUITOES MATTER TO US

First, let's be clear about why anyone would want this.  
*[VISUAL, scene 011: Pip walks toward camera along a dirt path with a notebook.]*

Mosquitoes are, by a wide margin, the deadliest animal to humans on the planet.  
*[VISUAL, scene 012: A lineup of animals on a podium: shark, snake, crocodile on lower steps; a tiny mosquito on the top step with a gold medal.]*

Not sharks. Not snakes. A fly smaller than your fingernail.  
*[VISUAL, scene 013: Pip's mitten hand with a mosquito standing on the fingertip, for scale.]*

Malaria alone kills around six hundred thousand people every year, according to the World Health Organization. Most of them are young children in Africa.  
*[VISUAL, scene 014: Map of Africa in muted ochre with a pulsing red-orange heat area across sub-Saharan Africa; small icon figures appear.]*

Then there's dengue. In 2024, countries reported more than fourteen million cases, the highest number ever recorded.  
*[VISUAL, scene 015: A bar chart drawn on a chalkboard, the last bar labelled 2024 shooting far above the others. Aedes mosquito perched on top.]*

Add Zika, yellow fever, West Nile virus, chikungunya, and you start to understand why some scientists say a world without mosquitoes would simply be safer for us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 016: Four labelled specimen jars on a shelf each with a different virus doodle; Pip nods.]*

But here's the twist. Most mosquitoes don't care about you at all.  
*[VISUAL, scene 017: A mosquito sipping from a flower, ignoring Pip who stands nearby with arms out, confused.]*

## THE MOSQUITOES WE NEVER THINK ABOUT

Of those three and a half thousand species, only a small fraction bite humans. Estimates range from around a hundred to a few hundred.  
*[VISUAL, scene 018: A giant grid of tiny mosquito icons; a small cluster turns red.]*

And only a few dozen spread serious disease.  
*[VISUAL, scene 019: Same grid, now only a tiny handful remain red.]*

And even among the biters, only the females drink blood. They need the protein to make eggs.  
*[VISUAL, scene 020: Female mosquito with a tiny egg raft beside her on water.]*

The males? They spend their whole lives sipping nectar from flowers.  
*[VISUAL, scene 021: Male mosquito (feathery antennae) relaxing on a flower, sipping nectar.]*

So when we say delete every mosquito, we're not just deleting the killers. We're deleting thousands of species that have never touched a human being.  
*[VISUAL, scene 022: Pip holding a giant eraser over a page full of mosquito drawings, hesitating.]*

What were all of them doing?  
*[VISUAL, scene 023: Pip looks up thoughtfully, finger on chin.]*

## THE WATER

To find out, we have to look somewhere most people never do: underwater.  
*[VISUAL, scene 024: Pip crouching at the edge of a pond, peering into the water with a magnifier.]*

A mosquito spends the first part of its life as a larva, a little wriggling worm hanging upside down from the surface of a pond, a puddle, or even the water trapped in an old tire.  
*[VISUAL, scene 025: Cross-section of water: larvae hanging from the surface by breathing tubes; inset of an old tyre holding rainwater.]*

These larvae are filter feeders. They eat decaying leaves, bacteria and tiny bits of organic matter, helping recycle nutrients in the water.  
*[VISUAL, scene 026: Close-up larva with tiny particles flowing toward its mouth brushes.]*

And they're food. Fish, dragonfly nymphs, beetles and tadpoles all eat mosquito larvae.  
*[VISUAL, scene 027: A small fish, a dragonfly nymph and a tadpole approach the larvae.]*

So the morning after, in millions of ponds around the world, a menu item has vanished.  
*[VISUAL, scene 028: The same underwater scene, larvae gone; the fish looks around puzzled.]*

Would the fish starve? Probably not. Most of these predators eat many other things too.  
*[VISUAL, scene 029: Fish happily eating a different small larva and a water flea.]*

Midges, other fly larvae and small crustaceans would likely fill much of the gap.  
*[VISUAL, scene 030: New tiny creatures drift into the empty space.]*

But in some small, isolated habitats, like the tiny pools of water inside pitcher plants, mosquito larvae are part of a very small food web, and the effects there are much harder to predict.  
*[VISUAL, scene 031: Pitcher plant cut-away showing a miniature pool with larvae; a question mark hovers.]*

## THE ARCTIC

Now let's travel north, to the Arctic tundra.  
*[VISUAL, scene 032: Map transition: dotted line travels north to the Arctic; Pip in a parka over the vest, hat on.]*

Here, for a few weeks each summer, mosquitoes emerge from melting snow pools in swarms so thick they can darken the sky.  
*[VISUAL, scene 033: Huge dark swarm cloud over tundra pools.]*

For migratory birds that fly thousands of kilometres to nest here, all those insects are a feast.  
*[VISUAL, scene 034: Small shorebirds catching insects in the air.]*

One estimate suggested that tundra bird numbers could fall by more than half without mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 035: Flock of birds; half fade away.]*

But other researchers point out that midges show up in bird stomachs far more often. Nobody knows for sure.  
*[VISUAL, scene 036: Scientist B shrugs holding a notebook, a midge and mosquito icon side by side.]*

Then there are the caribou. Swarms of mosquitoes harass them so badly that the herds move uphill and onto snow patches just to escape, sometimes giving up good grazing to do it.  
*[VISUAL, scene 037: Caribou herd walking uphill onto a snow patch, mosquito clouds behind; green grazing left behind below.]*

Remove the mosquitoes, and the caribou could eat in peace.  
*[VISUAL, scene 038: Caribou grazing calmly on green tundra.]*

Their movement could change. And whatever changes for a herd of caribou spreads to the wolves that hunt them, and the plants they trample and graze.  
*[VISUAL, scene 039: Simple diagram: caribou centre, arrows to wolves and plants.]*

A world without mosquitoes isn't automatically a world with fewer animals. In some places, it might even be a world with healthier ones.  
*[VISUAL, scene 040: Pip in parka watching a healthy caribou calf.]*

## THE FLOWERS

But there's one relationship that could genuinely be lost.  
*[VISUAL, scene 041: Pip walking into a cool northern forest, dim light.]*

In the cool forests and bogs of northern North America grows a small green orchid called the blunt-leaf orchid.  
*[VISUAL, scene 042: A small green orchid with a single broad leaf.]*

And its main visitors, are mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 043: A mosquito lands on the orchid.]*

In 2019, researchers found that the orchid's scent attracts mosquitoes, mostly Aedes species, which drink its nectar and carry its pollen from flower to flower.  
*[VISUAL, scene 044: Mosquito with tiny yellow pollen packets on its head flying between two orchids; scent wisps.]*

Mosquitoes aren't major pollinators of our crops. Bees, flies and moths do most of that work.  
*[VISUAL, scene 045: Crop field with bees, a hoverfly and a moth; the mosquito stands aside.]*

But for a handful of plants like this orchid, losing mosquitoes could mean losing their best partners.  
*[VISUAL, scene 046: The orchid alone, slightly drooping, with an empty space where the mosquito was.]*

## HUMANS

And then there's us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 047: Pip in a town square.]*

The morning after, the biggest change wouldn't be in the forest. It would be in hospitals.  
*[VISUAL, scene 048: Hospital ward with empty, made-up beds.]*

No new malaria infections. Ever. No dengue outbreaks. No Zika.  
*[VISUAL, scene 049: Three stamps: MALARIA, DENGUE, ZIKA each stamped with a big check-mark 'CLEARED'.]*

Year after year, hundreds of thousands of people, most of them children, who would have died, simply wouldn't.  
*[VISUAL, scene 050: Children running and playing in a village schoolyard.]*

Billions spent every year on bed nets, sprays and treatments could go elsewhere.  
*[VISUAL, scene 051: Stacks of bed nets and spray cans transform into school books and tools.]*

Some regions that were once too dangerous to farm or settle could open up.  
*[VISUAL, scene 052: A lush tropical valley with a new road being drawn into it.]*

And that could bring its own pressure: more land clearing, more roads, more people in wild places that mosquito-borne disease had, by accident, kept quiet.  
*[VISUAL, scene 053: Same valley, trees thinning as roads and fields spread.]*

Some ecologists have suggested that in a few corners of the world, the mosquito may have been one of nature's most effective park rangers.  
*[VISUAL, scene 054: A mosquito wearing a tiny ranger hat saluting in front of a rainforest.]*

## COULD WE DO IT?

So is this just a thought experiment? Not entirely.  
*[VISUAL, scene 055: Pip enters a modern lab.]*

In laboratories, scientists have built a gene drive, a genetic change that spreads through a population faster than normal inheritance allows.  
*[VISUAL, scene 056: Family-tree diagram of mosquitoes where a glowing gene spreads to every descendant.]*

In large indoor cages, it wiped out populations of Anopheles gambiae, one of the main malaria mosquitoes, in less than a year.  
*[VISUAL, scene 057: Large mesh cage; mosquito population inside thins to zero over a calendar flipping.]*

But notice the target: not all mosquitoes. One species. Maybe a few.  
*[VISUAL, scene 058: Magnifier over a lineup of many mosquitoes, highlighting just one.]*

Because that's where most researchers have landed. We don't need to erase three and a half thousand species to save lives. We need to stop the few dozen that make us sick.  
*[VISUAL, scene 059: Scientists A and B nodding at a board showing a big circle (all species) with a tiny red slice.]*

And the gene drive is still being tested. It hasn't been released into the wild.  
*[VISUAL, scene 060: A locked cage with a 'TESTING' tag.]*

So what would happen if every mosquito disappeared tonight?

The skies over the Arctic would be quieter.  
*[VISUAL, scene 062: Calm tundra sky at dusk.]*

A small orchid might struggle to find a partner.

And somewhere, hundreds of thousands of children would grow up who otherwise never would have.

The harder question isn't whether the world could survive without mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 067: Pip looking at a single mosquito in a jar.]*
