## WHY MOSQUITOES MATTER TO US

First, let's be clear about why anyone would want this.  
*[VISUAL, scene 011: Pip walks along a dirt path between fields, flipping open a notebook.]*

Mosquitoes are, by a wide margin, the deadliest animal to humans on the planet.  
*[VISUAL, scene 012: A three-step winners' podium on a sky-blue backdrop: shark and snake on the lower steps, a tiny mosquito on the top step receiving a gold medal.]*

Not sharks. Not snakes. A fly smaller than your fingernail.  
*[VISUAL, scene 013: Pip's large mitten hand with a mosquito standing on the fingertip, for scale.]*

Malaria alone kills around six hundred thousand people every year, according to the World Health Organization. Most of them are young children in Africa.  
*[VISUAL, scene 014: Stylised map of Africa in muted ochre on a dark-teal sea, with a pulsing red-orange zone across sub-Saharan Africa; small person icons fade in.]*

Then there's dengue. In 2024, countries reported more than fourteen million cases, the highest number ever recorded.  
*[VISUAL, scene 015: A chalkboard bar chart. The last bar, labelled 2024, shoots far above the others; an Aedes mosquito perches on top of it.]*

Add Zika, yellow fever, West Nile virus, chikungunya, and you start to understand why some scientists say a world without mosquitoes would simply be safer for us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 016: Four specimen jars on a lab shelf, each holding a different colourful virus doodle; they light up one by one while Pip nods beside them.]*

But here's the twist. Most mosquitoes don't care about you at all.  
*[VISUAL, scene 017: A mosquito sips nectar from a flower, ignoring Pip who stands next to it with arms out, mouth open in surprise.]*

## HOW A MOSQUITO FINDS YOU

Part of what makes the dangerous ones so dangerous is how good they are at finding us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 018: Dusk backyard. Pip walks across the lawn; a single mosquito quietly follows behind.]*

Every time you breathe out, you release a plume of carbon dioxide. A hungry female can pick up that trail from several metres away, and follow it upwind.  
*[VISUAL, scene 019: Side view: Pip exhales a soft, visible wavy plume that drifts across the frame; a mosquito at the far end turns and follows the plume toward Pip.]*

As she gets closer, she switches senses. She smells the chemicals in your sweat and skin. Then, in the last few dozen centimetres, she can feel the heat coming off your body.  
*[VISUAL, scene 020: Close on Pip's arm: green scent wisps rise from it, then orange heat waves shimmer; the mosquito's antennae twitch as it closes in.]*

Breath, smell, heat. Three clues, one target.  
*[VISUAL, scene 021: Three round icons (breath cloud, scent wisps, thermometer) pop in one by one and fly together into a target circle around Pip.]*

## AN ANCIENT SURVIVOR

Mosquitoes are also survivors. They were buzzing around long before us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 022: Pip holds a large golden amber stone up to the light, squinting at it through round glasses.]*

Mosquitoes have been found preserved in amber that is around ninety-nine million years old, from the age of the dinosaurs.  
*[VISUAL, scene 023: Close-up of a glowing amber stone with a tiny mosquito frozen inside; a long-necked dinosaur silhouette walks past in the background.]*

And in Montana, scientists found a forty-six-million-year-old fossil mosquito whose belly still held traces of the blood from its last meal.  
*[VISUAL, scene 024: A grey shale slab with a flattened fossil mosquito; its belly glows faint red; Scientist A leans in with a magnifier.]*

They have lived through ice ages, asteroid impacts and shifting continents. Which makes it all the stranger to imagine them gone overnight.  
*[VISUAL, scene 025: A long timeline strip: an asteroid, drifting continents, an ice sheet; a cartoon mosquito hops from panel to panel, then vanishes in a puff at the end.]*

## THE MOSQUITOES WE NEVER THINK ABOUT

Of those three and a half thousand species, only a small fraction bite humans. Estimates range from around a hundred to a few hundred.  
*[VISUAL, scene 026: A giant grid of tiny mosquito icons on cream paper; a small cluster turns red.]*

And only a few dozen spread serious disease.  
*[VISUAL, scene 027: The same grid; almost all red icons fade back, leaving only a tiny handful red.]*

And even among the biters, only the females drink blood. They need the protein to make eggs.  
*[VISUAL, scene 028: A female mosquito standing on a water surface next to a small raft of eggs that appear one by one.]*

The males? They spend their whole lives sipping nectar from flowers.  
*[VISUAL, scene 029: A male mosquito with bushy feathery antennae relaxes on a big flower, sipping nectar, eyes half-closed in bliss.]*

So when we say delete every mosquito, we're not just deleting the killers. We're deleting thousands of species that have never touched a human being.  
*[VISUAL, scene 030: Top-down desk: a page full of mosquito drawings; Pip's hand holds a giant pink eraser hovering over them, hesitating.]*

What were all of them doing?  
*[VISUAL, scene 031: Pip looks up thoughtfully, mitten on chin, a question mark floating beside the hat.]*

## THE WATER

To find out, we have to look somewhere most people never do: underwater.  
*[VISUAL, scene 032: Pip crouches at the edge of a pond, peering in with a magnifying glass.]*

Every mosquito starts life in water. A female lays her eggs on or near the surface, sometimes hundreds at a time.  
*[VISUAL, scene 033: A female mosquito hovers just above calm water, laying a neat raft of eggs that grows on the surface.]*

Some eggs can even survive drying out for months, waiting for the next rain.  
*[VISUAL, scene 034: An old bucket with a dry cracked bottom and tiny eggs stuck to the wall; rain begins, the bucket fills, eggs wobble.]*

The eggs hatch into larvae, little wriggling worms hanging upside down from the surface of a pond, a puddle, or even the water trapped in an old tire.  
*[VISUAL, scene 035: Underwater cross-section: larvae hang from the surface by breathing tubes; an inset circle shows an old tyre holding rainwater.]*

These larvae are filter feeders. They eat decaying leaves, bacteria and tiny bits of organic matter, helping recycle nutrients in the water.  
*[VISUAL, scene 036: Close-up of one larva with tiny particles and a leaf fragment drifting toward its brushy mouth.]*

After a few moults, the larva becomes a pupa, a comma-shaped capsule that tumbles through the water.  
*[VISUAL, scene 037: A comma-shaped pupa with two little breathing horns tumbles down and floats back up.]*

Then it splits open, and an adult climbs out onto the surface and flies away. In warm weather, the whole journey from egg to adult can take just a week or two.  
*[VISUAL, scene 038: At the water surface, a pupa splits and a new adult mosquito climbs out, dries its wings and flies off; a small calendar flips pages.]*

And they're food. Fish, dragonfly nymphs, beetles and tadpoles all eat mosquito larvae.  
*[VISUAL, scene 039: Underwater: a small fish, a dragonfly nymph and a tadpole approach the hanging larvae.]*

So the morning after, in millions of ponds around the world, a menu item has vanished.  
*[VISUAL, scene 040: The same underwater scene, larvae gone; the fish looks around puzzled.]*

Would the fish starve? Probably not. Most of these predators eat many other things too.  
*[VISUAL, scene 041: The fish happily gulps a different small larva and a water flea.]*

Midges, other fly larvae and small crustaceans would likely fill much of the gap.  
*[VISUAL, scene 042: New tiny creatures drift into the empty space where mosquito larvae used to hang.]*

But in some small, isolated habitats, like the tiny pools of water inside pitcher plants, mosquito larvae are part of a very small food web, and the effects there are much harder to predict.  
*[VISUAL, scene 043: A cut-away pitcher plant showing a miniature pool with larvae; a question mark hovers above.]*

## THE NIGHT SHIFT

Once they take to the air, adult mosquitoes become food too.  
*[VISUAL, scene 044: Dusk sky over a meadow with a loose cloud of mosquitoes rising from the grass.]*

Dragonflies snatch them mid-flight. Spiders catch them in webs. Swallows, swifts and many small birds scoop them out of the air.  
*[VISUAL, scene 045: A dragonfly zips through the swarm, a spider web glistens in the corner, and a swallow swoops across.]*

And then there are bats. You may have heard that a single bat eats thousands of mosquitoes a night.  
*[VISUAL, scene 046: Night sky with a moon; a bat flaps through a cloud of mosquitoes with a big question mark beside it.]*

The truth is more interesting. When scientists checked the DNA in bat droppings, they did find mosquitoes, often. But for most bats, moths and beetles make up a much bigger part of the menu.  
*[VISUAL, scene 047: Scientist B beside a large dinner plate chart: a big moth and a big beetle fill the plate, a tiny mosquito sits on the rim.]*

For the hunters of the night, mosquitoes are more like a snack than a main course.  
*[VISUAL, scene 048: The bat holding a moth like a sandwich, a tiny mosquito on the side like a crisp.]*

## THE ARCTIC

Now let's travel north, to the Arctic tundra.  
*[VISUAL, scene 049: Map transition: a dotted line travels north to the Arctic; Pip appears on tundra wearing a parka over the vest.]*

Here, for a few weeks each summer, mosquitoes emerge from melting snow pools in swarms so thick they can darken the sky.  
*[VISUAL, scene 050: A huge dark swarm cloud rises over tundra pools.]*

For migratory birds that fly thousands of kilometres to nest here, all those insects are a feast.  
*[VISUAL, scene 051: Small shorebirds catching insects in the air above the tundra.]*

One estimate suggested that tundra bird numbers could fall by more than half without mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 052: A flock of birds; half of them fade away.]*

But other researchers point out that midges show up in bird stomachs far more often. Nobody knows for sure.  
*[VISUAL, scene 053: Scientist B shrugs holding a notebook; a midge icon and a mosquito icon float side by side.]*

Then there are the caribou. Swarms of mosquitoes harass them so badly that the herds move uphill and onto snow patches just to escape, sometimes giving up good grazing to do it.  
*[VISUAL, scene 054: A caribou herd walks uphill onto a snow patch, mosquito clouds behind them; green grazing left below.]*

Remove the mosquitoes, and the caribou could eat in peace.  
*[VISUAL, scene 055: Caribou grazing calmly on green tundra.]*

Their movement could change. And whatever changes for a herd of caribou spreads to the wolves that hunt them, and the plants they trample and graze.  
*[VISUAL, scene 056: Simple diagram on paper: caribou in the centre, arrows drawing out to a wolf and to plants.]*

A world without mosquitoes isn't automatically a world with fewer animals. In some places, it might even be a world with healthier ones.  
*[VISUAL, scene 057: Pip in a parka watching a healthy caribou calf hop.]*

## THE FLOWERS

But there's one relationship that could genuinely be lost.  
*[VISUAL, scene 058: Pip walks into a cool northern forest of dark spruce, dim light.]*

In the cool forests and bogs of northern North America grows a small green orchid called the blunt-leaf orchid.  
*[VISUAL, scene 059: A small green orchid with a single broad leaf on moss.]*

And its main visitors, are mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 060: A mosquito lands on the orchid.]*

In 2019, researchers found that the orchid's scent attracts mosquitoes, mostly Aedes species, which drink its nectar and carry its pollen from flower to flower.  
*[VISUAL, scene 061: A mosquito with tiny yellow pollen packets on its head flies between two orchids along scent wisps.]*

Mosquitoes aren't major pollinators of our crops. Bees, flies and moths do most of that work.  
*[VISUAL, scene 062: A crop field with a bee, a hoverfly and a moth busy on flowers; the mosquito stands aside.]*

But for a handful of plants like this orchid, losing mosquitoes could mean losing their best partners.  
*[VISUAL, scene 063: The orchid alone at dusk, slightly drooping, an empty space where the mosquito was.]*

## HUMANS

And then there's us.  
*[VISUAL, scene 064: Pip in a small-town square turns to camera.]*

The morning after, the biggest change wouldn't be in the forest. It would be in hospitals.  
*[VISUAL, scene 065: A hospital ward with empty, neatly made beds as morning light comes on.]*

No new malaria infections. Ever. No dengue outbreaks. No Zika.  
*[VISUAL, scene 066: Three file cards labelled MALARIA, DENGUE, ZIKA each get slammed with a green check-mark stamp.]*

Year after year, hundreds of thousands of people, most of them children, who would have died, simply wouldn't.  
*[VISUAL, scene 067: Children running and playing in a village schoolyard.]*

Billions spent every year on bed nets, sprays and treatments could go elsewhere.  
*[VISUAL, scene 068: Stacks of bed nets and spray cans transform into school books and tools.]*

Some regions that were once too dangerous to farm or settle could open up.  
*[VISUAL, scene 069: A lush tropical valley with a new road drawing itself into it.]*

And that could bring its own pressure: more land clearing, more roads, more people in wild places that mosquito-borne disease had, by accident, kept quiet.  
*[VISUAL, scene 070: Same valley; trees thin out as roads and fields spread.]*

Some ecologists have suggested that in a few corners of the world, the mosquito may have been one of nature's most effective park rangers.  
*[VISUAL, scene 071: A mosquito wearing a tiny ranger hat salutes in front of a rainforest.]*

## WE'VE TRIED THIS BEFORE

Humans have been at war with mosquitoes for a long time, and we've learned some hard lessons.  
*[VISUAL, scene 072: Pip in front of a wall of sepia framed pictures (a ship, a canal, a spray can).]*

In the 1880s, France tried to dig a canal across Panama. Yellow fever and malaria tore through the workforce, and by the time the project collapsed, an estimated twenty thousand people had died.  
*[VISUAL, scene 073: Sepia map of the Isthmus of Panama with a dotted canal line that stops halfway; small worker figures fade away.]*

When the United States took over in 1904, an army doctor named William Gorgas tried something new. Instead of fighting the disease, he went after the mosquito.  
*[VISUAL, scene 074: Sepia: a doctor stick figure with a moustache and an old-style hat points firmly at a large mosquito drawing on a board.]*

His teams drained standing water, covered water barrels, and put screens on windows. Within about two years, yellow fever was gone from the canal zone.  
*[VISUAL, scene 075: Sepia: workers put a lid on a water barrel and fit a mesh screen on a window; a big green check-mark appears.]*

Then came D D T. In 1955, the World Health Organization launched a global campaign to wipe out malaria, by spraying insecticide inside millions of homes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 076: A worker with a backpack sprayer sprays mist on the wall of a simple house; a globe icon spins in the corner.]*

It worked in many places. But mosquitoes evolved resistance, funding dried up, and much of tropical Africa was never fully included. In 1969, the eradication plan was abandoned.  
*[VISUAL, scene 077: Mosquitoes fly happily through a spray cloud unharmed; a calendar flips to 1969 and a red 'ABANDONED' stamp lands.]*

The lesson? Mosquitoes adapt. Any plan to stop them has to be smarter than they are.  
*[VISUAL, scene 078: Pip taps temple thoughtfully; a mosquito opposite also taps its head.]*

## COULD WE DO IT?

So is this just a thought experiment? Not entirely.  
*[VISUAL, scene 079: Pip opens the door of a modern lab.]*

One idea already works on city streets. Scientists infect Aedes mosquitoes with a common bacterium called Wolbachia, which makes them much worse at spreading dengue.  
*[VISUAL, scene 080: A big friendly mosquito with glowing green dots inside its body (the bacteria); a dengue virus doodle bounces off it.]*

In a trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, neighbourhoods where these mosquitoes were released had seventy-seven percent fewer dengue cases.  
*[VISUAL, scene 081: A stylised city map split into blocks: released blocks turn green; a bar chart shows a tall bar shrinking by 77 percent.]*

Other teams release swarms of sterile or modified males. They mate with wild females, but no new generation survives.  
*[VISUAL, scene 082: A release box opens and male mosquitoes (feathery antennae) fly out; an egg raft below fades with a small cross.]*

In laboratories, scientists have also built a gene drive, a genetic change that spreads through a population faster than normal inheritance allows.  
*[VISUAL, scene 083: A family-tree diagram of mosquito icons on a whiteboard; a glowing gene spreads down to every descendant.]*

In large indoor cages, it wiped out populations of Anopheles gambiae, one of the main malaria mosquitoes, in less than a year.  
*[VISUAL, scene 084: A large mesh cage; the mosquito population inside thins to zero while a calendar flips.]*

But notice the target: not all mosquitoes. One species. Maybe a few.  
*[VISUAL, scene 085: A magnifying glass passes over a lineup of many mosquito species, highlighting just one.]*

Because that's where most researchers have landed. We don't need to erase three and a half thousand species to save lives. We need to stop the few dozen that make us sick.  
*[VISUAL, scene 086: Scientists A and B nod at a board showing a big circle (all species) with a tiny red slice.]*

And the gene drive is still being tested. It hasn't been released into the wild.  
*[VISUAL, scene 087: A locked cage with a swinging 'TESTING' tag.]*

## THE RISKS

There's a good reason to be careful. A gene drive is designed to spread on its own. Once released, it can't easily be called back, and mosquitoes don't respect borders.  
*[VISUAL, scene 088: A map with dashed country borders; a green glow spreads outward across the borders.]*

That's why scientists, governments and the communities who live with these mosquitoes are still debating how, and whether, to use it.  
*[VISUAL, scene 089: A community meeting under a tree: villagers, a scientist and an official sit in a circle, nodding and gesturing.]*

## CONCLUSION

So what would happen if every mosquito disappeared tonight?  
*[VISUAL, scene 090: Night bedroom again (callback), clock near midnight, Pip sitting up awake.]*

The skies over the Arctic would be quieter.  
*[VISUAL, scene 091: Calm tundra sky at dusk, clouds drifting.]*

A few fish and birds would change their menus.  
*[VISUAL, scene 092: A fish and a bird each eating something new.]*

A small orchid might struggle to find a partner.  
*[VISUAL, scene 093: Lonely orchid at dusk.]*

And somewhere, hundreds of thousands of children would grow up who otherwise never would have.  
*[VISUAL, scene 094: Children growing taller in a time-lapse at a school doorway.]*

Nature would almost certainly adapt. Something else would slip into the empty spaces, as it always does.  
*[VISUAL, scene 095: Pond scene bustling with other insects.]*

The harder question isn't whether the world could survive without mosquitoes.  
*[VISUAL, scene 096: Pip at a desk looking at a single mosquito in a jar under a lamp.]*

It's whether we're brave enough, and careful enough, to decide which ones it should live without.  
*[VISUAL, scene 097: Pip opens the jar at the window and the mosquito flies out into the night.]*
